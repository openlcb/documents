                                                1 Introduction

   A Working Note is an intermediate step in the documentation process.  It gathers together the content
   from various informal development documents, discussions, etc into a single place.  One or more Working
   Notes form the basic for the next step, which is one or more Standard/TechNote pairs.

   This working note describes a OpenLCB protocol for a detector to report its detection of a train,
   including the DCC address of an associated DCC decoder.

   “Detector” refers to a fixed-location electronic device that can interact with a DCC decoder to
   ascertain the address of the DCC decoder while the decoder is in a specific area or at a specific point.
   Possible implementations include but are not limited to RailCom detectors, Digitrax detectors, RFID and
   QR readers.

1.1 Served Use Cases

   A train enters a current-detected block.  A detector recognizes this, successfully queries the decoder
   for its address, and reports that information on the OpenLCB bus. That information results in a signal
   controller sending a speed command such as Slow or Stop to that particular detector.

   A train crosses an RFID reader which determines the train’s DCC address and reports that address on the
   OpenLCB bus.

   A train exits a current-detected block. A detector recognizes this, and reports that interaction on the
   OpenLCB bus.

   A train enters a current detected block. A detector recognizes this, and attempts unsuccessfully to
   query the decoder for its address.  The detector emits a report without decoder address information.

1.2 Unserved Use Cases

   Being able to report information on a native OpenLCB (non-DCC) train is an unserved use case. This
   protocol doesn’t provide a way to report the Unique ID of a fully OpenLCB train.  All devices being
   reported need to have a DCC address.

   Having a monitor tool that can show all detection information without configuration is a unserved use
   case. The Unique IDs of the detectors, hence the upper bytes of Event IDs to be consumed, are determined
   by a pre-configuration process in the consumer.  They can’t be determined automatically.

2.1 Introduction

   Note that this section of the Standard is informative, not normative.

2.2 Intended Use

   Note that this section of the Standard is informative, not normative.

   This protocol is intended to provide a standard way to transfer train location and DCC decoder
   identification information on OpenLCB DCC layouts.

2.3 Reference and Context

   This protocol builds on the Event Transport Protocol as defined by that Standard and Technical Note.

2.4 Message Formats

   Detection events are produced via PCER messages with an Event ID which contains

    1. 6 byte unique ID of the detector,

    2. Two bits to represent entry/exit and orientation.  These are coded:

   0b00 unoccupied, i.e., exit

   0b01 occupied, facing West, i.e., entry

   0b10 occupied, facing East, i.e., entry

   0b11 occupied, orientation unknown, i.e., entry

    1. 14 bits of DCC address

    1.   1. If the high bits are 0x38 this represents a DCC short address

         2. If the high bits are 0x39 this represents a DCC consist address

         3. An address of 0x3800 (short address 0) represents that the track is empty, i.e. there is no
            decoder present.

         4. Otherwise it’s a DCC long address

   The encoding of DCC addresses into 14 bits follows the S-9.2.1.1 and RCN-218 standards.

   The 0x3800 value is used for track empty instead of 0x0000 because 0x0000 is long address zero, which is
   a valid (though uncommon) long address.

   A detector may, but does not have to, use the 0x3800 “track is empty” by for example producing a “'track
   is empty' leaves” event when the track becomes occupied, and a “‘track is empty arrives” when the track
   becomes unoccupied.  For example:

    

   06.80.99.30.03.50.38.00 - "track is empty” leaves

   06.80.99.30.03.50.F8.03 - 3S arrives

   06.80.99.30.03.50.38.03 - 3S leaves

   06.80.99.30.03.50.F8.00 - "track is empty" arrives

  2.4.1 Orientation Definitions

   Detectors that report orientation (such as RailCom detectors) determine the physical orientation of the
   decoder on the track, rather than the internal throttle direction ("forward" or "reverse") commanded to
   the locomotive motor. Following DCC standards, the two detectable track orientations are specified as
   "facing West" and "facing East".

   The normative definitions of "West" and "East" are taken from RailCommunity standard RCN-217
   (Bi-directional communication, Section 5.5, Endnote 5):

   „West“ ist definiert als bei positiver Polarität des Gleissignals nach [RCN-210] Abschnitt 2.3 vorwärts
   und rückwärts bei negativer Polarität. „Ost“ ist entsprechend umgekehrt definiert.

   (Translation: "West" is defined as forward movement with positive polarity of the track signal according
   to [RCN-210] Section 2.3, and reverse movement with negative polarity. "East" is correspondingly defined
   vice versa.)

   RCN-210 Section 2.3 establishes the electrical and wiring polarity:

    1. The red decoder wire connects to the right-hand rail (facing forward from the cab).

    2. The black decoder wire connects to the left-hand rail.

    3. "Positive polarity" is defined as the right rail being positive with respect to the left rail during
       the first half of the DCC bit cycle: V(right rail - left rail) > 0.

   Standard Layout Reference:

    1. For track running East-West, the North rail is wired as Rail A (positive polarity in the first half
       of the DCC bit), and the South rail is wired as Rail B. In a common depiction, this means that on
       the track in front of you leftwards is Westbound, rightwards is Eastbound, the back rail (the rail
       that is further away from you) is Rail A, wired with red wire.

    2. A locomotive facing West has its right rail on the North rail (Rail A). In forward motion, its right
       rail is positive, satisfying the RCN-217 definition for "West". In reverse motion facing East, its
       right rail is Rail B (negative), which also evaluates to "West".

    3. A locomotive facing East has its right rail on the South rail (Rail B). In forward motion, its right
       rail is negative, satisfying the RCN-217 definition for "East".

   Translation to RailCom Detector Hardware:

    1. During the RailCom cutout, RailCom detectors measure current flow between Rail A and Rail B:

         1. When the North rail (Rail A) sources RailCom current and the South rail (Rail B) sinks
            current—producing a positive voltage across the detector measurement resistor on (Rail A - Rail
            B)—the locomotive is detected as facing West (code 0b01).

         2. When the South rail (Rail B) sources RailCom current and the North rail (Rail A) sinks
            current—producing a negative voltage on (Rail A - Rail B)—the locomotive is detected as facing
            East (code 0b10).

   Unknown orientation needs to be used in a variety of situations:

     * If a RailCom detector is wired on a three-rail layout (where direction of current is fixed / does
       not correlate with orientation).

     * If a RailCom detector is not equipped with hardware for distinguishing positive / negative current
       flow direction.

     * If the detector uses a technology (e.g. RFID) that represents DCC address but does not represent
       orientation.

   Bit Mapping:

   0b01 Occupied, facing West

   0b10 Occupied, facing East

   0b11 Occupied, orientation unknown (used when the detector can read the decoder address, but cannot
   determine track orientation)

   Examples of Orientation Events:

   For a detector with Node ID 06.80.99.30.03.50 detecting locomotive short address 3 (0x3803):

   06.80.99.30.03.50.78.03 - Locomotive 3S arrives, facing West (0b01)

   06.80.99.30.03.50.B8.03 - Locomotive 3S arrives, facing East (0b10)

   06.80.99.30.03.50.F8.03 - Locomotive 3S arrives, orientation unknown (0b11)

   06.80.99.30.03.50.38.03 - Locomotive 3S leaves (0b00)

2.5 States

   This protocol has no states of its own.

   An “exit Event ID can be sent at most once after a corresponding entry Event ID” state requirement was
   discussed but not accepted. Note that some hardware detectors determine only that a decoder has left the
   monitored section, not its address, so those scanners will be producing “NNN entry” followed by “(none)
   exit”.

2.6 Interactions

   A detector sends a PCER with an Event ID as described above when it detects entry of a decoder into the
   monitored location. It may also send a PCER when the exit of the decoder from the monitored location is
   detected.

   A detector, acting as a producer, must reply to Verify Events and Identify Producers messages with the
   appropriate event(s) or range.

   Consumers can consume the entire 16-bit range of the lower EventID bytes. Or they can consume just the
   specific EventIDs that correspond to the particular decoder(s) that they’re tracking.  In either case,
   they must respond to Verify Events and Identify Consumers messages with the appropriate event(s) or
   range.

   There is no PIP bit allocated to this protocol.

                                           3 Background Information

   This protocol has been implemented in several products. It is known to work for its served use cases.
